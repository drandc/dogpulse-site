Dog medication log (printable): how to track every dose

Free to print, takes two minutes to set up. Updated 25 November 2026.

A course of antibiotics, a daily pill for a heart or skin condition, a monthly flea and tick tablet, a joint supplement. Once a dog takes more than one thing, it gets hard to say whether this morning's dose was given, by whom, and when the next one is due.

A medication log fixes that. It takes two minutes to set up, it stops doses from being missed or given twice, and it gives your vet a clear record at the next visit. Print this page and fill it in, or copy the layout into your notes.

The short answer

A dog medication log should show, for every medicine, the name and strength, the dose, how often and how it is given, the start and end date, and when a refill is due. For every dose it should show the date, the time, who gave it and anything unusual, such as a spat-out pill or vomiting. Keep it where everyone who gives medicine can see it and tick it as soon as the dose is given.

Questions to ask your vet before you leave

Most medication mistakes start with something that was never asked. These questions come from the US Food and Drug Administration. Write the answers on your list.

Ask the vet to show you how to use any syringe or dropper, and ask for the instructions in writing.

Missed or spat-out doses

Do not guess. What to do about a missed dose depends on the medicine, so ask your vet when you collect it and write the answer next to that medicine on your list. Do not give a double dose to catch up unless your vet tells you to.

If you think you gave too much, or your dog got into the packet, call your vet or an animal poison helpline straight away. If your dog seems unwell after a dose, contact your vet right away and tell them exactly what was given and when. That is where a log earns its place.

Giving medicine safely

Sharing the job

When two people give medicine, the commonest mistake is a double dose, because each of you thought the other had not done it yet. Keep one log where both of you see it, for example on the fridge, and tick it the moment the dose is given.

If a sitter or boarding kennel will give the medicine, hand over this log with the medicines in their original packaging. Our dog sitter checklist covers the rest of what to leave with them.

Paper or phone?

Paper is fine for a short course, and a printed sheet by the pill box is hard to beat. A phone helps when the schedule is long, when there are several medicines, or when you are not always the one at home: it can remind you at the right time, record the dose with the time, and keep the history ready for the vet.

Whatever you use, choose one place for the record and keep it up to date.

Common questions

What should be in a dog medication log?

For each medicine: the name and strength, the dose, how often and how it is given, the start and end date and the refill date. For each dose: the date, the time, who gave it and anything unusual, such as a spat-out pill or vomiting. The log above has space for all of it.

How do I keep track of my dog's medications?

Keep one list of everything your dog takes, including supplements and flea, tick and worm treatments, and one log where each dose is ticked off as it is given. Put both where everyone who gives medicine can see them, on paper by the pills or in an app.

What should I do if I miss a dose of my dog's medication?

Ask your vet. The right answer depends on the medicine, so ask when you collect it and write the answer on your list. Do not give a double dose to catch up unless your vet tells you to, and call your vet at once if you think your dog got too much.

How do I remember to give my dog medicine on time?

Tie it to something you do every day, such as a meal or your morning coffee, set a phone alarm or app reminder, and tick the log straight away. A weekly pill organiser can help, but keep the medicines in their labelled original packaging as well.

What if my dog spits out or vomits the pill?

Ask your vet this before you leave the clinic, because the answer depends on the medicine and on how soon after the dose it happened. Write down what happened and when, and call the vet if you are unsure.

Can I give my dog human medicine?

Not unless your vet tells you to. Some common human medicines, including some painkillers, are dangerous to dogs. Keep your own medicines apart from your dog's.

What questions should I ask the vet about a new medication?

Ask why it is prescribed and for how long, how to give it and whether with food, how much and how often, how to store it, what to do about a missed or spat-out dose, which side effects to watch for, and whether to finish the whole course. The list above has them all.

Should I bring a medication list to the vet?

Yes. Bring a list of everything your dog takes, including supplements and over-the-counter products, with doses and start dates, and any past reactions. It helps your vet avoid interactions and gives a clear picture of what your dog has actually been given.
